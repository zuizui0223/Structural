# Cover letter — Global Ecology and Biogeography v1.184

Dear Editors of *Global Ecology and Biogeography*,

Please consider our Research Article, **“When do source islands matter? Topology without source irreplaceability.”**

**Why this paper should interest GEB readers (under 250 words).** Landscape connectivity studies commonly use network-level structure to identify critical patches, sources or corridors. We test whether that translation is empirically justified in a global island system. Across 5,401 islands, a preregistered layer of 529 mammal species with only 1–4 occupied pilot islands showed strong topology-specific held-out occurrence information: graph-path source terms improved realized-presence prediction (C−R3 = −0.596; 95% interval −0.744 to −0.464), and the observed island adjacency outperformed all 20 degree- and edge-length-matched rewired topologies. We then asked, without reopening any held-out occurrence, whether the occupied source islands that generated this signal divided target space into distinct, irreplaceable source territories. They did not. Among 212 species with 2–4 sources, source-identity turnover across target islands was substantially **lower** than in 1,000 source-count- and bioregion-matched random placements per species (actual−null beta = −0.262; 95% interval −0.317 to −0.208). Local effective source number was higher (+0.394) and target-level source dominance lower (−0.139). Exact topology therefore predicted occurrence as a collective source configuration even though individual source influence was more structurally redundant than random. This separates network-level ecological information from node-level conservation priority.

An independent three-wave Azores arthropod test adds a second boundary: source-loss leverage did not improve held-out prediction of later contraction. The manuscript therefore distinguishes three often-conflated claims—network topology matters, individual sources are irreplaceable, and source loss has future demographic consequence—and finds support only for the first in the strongest mammal layer.
