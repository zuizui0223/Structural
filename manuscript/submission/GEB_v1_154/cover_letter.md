# Cover letter — Global Ecology and Biogeography v1.154

Dear Editors of *Global Ecology and Biogeography*,

Please consider our Research Article, **“When do source islands matter? Occupancy state changes topology-specific isolation across islands.”**

Island biogeography has long treated isolation as a central control on colonization and extinction, while metapopulation theory already makes an important refinement: effective connectivity can depend on which potential source populations are occupied. We therefore do **not** present occupancy-dependent connectivity itself as a new concept. Instead, we ask a more specific macroecological question that can be tested across a fixed global island geography:

> **When does the exact arrangement of occupied conspecific source islands contain information beyond source number, source proximity and generic island geography?**

We analyse native mammals across 5,401 islands using three disjoint species layers while preserving unread held-out responses for prospective tests.

An exploratory layer of 79 species with at least 13 pilot presences showed a small graph-transformed source-context gain dominated by absences, but a matched-null audit showed that the observed island adjacency was not uniquely informative. A preregistered disjoint layer of 96 species with 5–12 pilot presences did not replicate that overall gain and likewise showed no topology specificity.

The resulting presence-opportunity hypothesis was then tested prospectively in a third, previously unread layer of **529 species with only 1–4 pilot presences**. Before any held-out response access, we froze the complete reference and candidate prediction surfaces together with 20 degree- and edge-length-matched rewired graph nulls. The preregistered primary was strongly supported: presence-cell C−R3 = **−0.596** (95% interval **−0.744 to −0.464**). The observed island adjacency also outperformed **all 20** matched null topologies on the preregistered presence metric.

The ecological result is therefore not a universal “connectivity matters” effect or a monotonic rarity gradient. It is an **occupancy-regime change in topology specificity**. For species occupying very few islands, the identity and arrangement of the remaining occupied sources contain held-out occurrence information beyond mainland isolation, occupancy breadth, regional prevalence, source number, direct source distance and generic island-network context.

We then tested the stronger conservation interpretation in an independent three-wave Azores arthropod system. Source-loss exposure was frozen from BALA1→BALA2 before BALA3 outcomes were opened. The temporal primary was not supported: adding target-specific lost-source leverage did not improve held-out prediction of subsequent contraction (C−R2 = **+0.000674**, 95% taxon-bootstrap interval **−0.02130 to +0.02558**).

We regard that negative result as part of the biological contribution rather than a result to rescue. It separates two quantities that are often conflated in connectivity-based conservation: **spatial distinctiveness in an occurrence network** and **future demographic consequence after a source population is lost**. Our results show the former in the sparsest mammal occupancy regime, but do not establish the latter.

The manuscript is explicit that graph edges are not demonstrated dispersal routes, occurrence patterns do not demonstrate rescue, “ultrarare” refers only to island occupancy in the frozen pilot data, and static source leverage is not validated as a management-priority score.

We believe the combination of global island scale, disjoint prospective species layers, matched topology nulls, and an independent temporal falsification test makes the manuscript well suited to *Global Ecology and Biogeography*: it asks when physical geography is an adequate description of isolation, and when the focal species' remaining source configuration adds information that geography alone does not.
