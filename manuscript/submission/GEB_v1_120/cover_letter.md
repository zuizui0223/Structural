# Cover letter — Global Ecology and Biogeography v1.120

Dear Editors of *Global Ecology and Biogeography*,

Please consider our Research Article, **“When do source islands matter? Species occupancy changes the role of connectivity across islands.”**

Island biogeography has long linked isolation to colonization from source regions, and the rescue effect further recognizes that immigration from conspecific sources can reduce local extinction. Yet connectivity metrics are usually treated as properties of islands or landscapes. Our study asks a more species-centred question: **does the ecological information carried by occupied source islands change as a species occupies fewer islands?**

We analyse native mammals across 5,401 islands using three disjoint species layers defined from the same pilot islands while preserving unread held-out responses for later prospective tests.

An initial exploratory layer of 79 species with at least 13 pilot presences showed a broad natural-prevalence improvement from graph-path occupied-source information, but the gain was driven mainly by absences and a later matched-null audit showed that the observed adjacency was not uniquely informative.

We then preregistered a disjoint 96-species layer with 5–12 pilot presences before opening its 396,096 held-out responses. That layer did not replicate the overall gain and showed no topology specificity, although realized-presence prediction improved strongly.

The resulting presence-opportunity hypothesis was tested prospectively in a third, previously unread layer of **529 species with only 1–4 pilot presences**. Before any held-out response access, we froze the complete R3/C prediction surface and 20 degree- and edge-length-matched rewired graph nulls. The preregistered primary was strongly supported: presence-cell C−R3 = **−0.596** (95% interval **−0.744 to −0.464**). The benefit was stronger where a graph-reachable occupied source existed, and the actual island adjacency outperformed **all 20** matched null topologies on the preregistered presence metric.

We believe this result has a direct island-biogeographic interpretation. For species occupying very few islands, the remaining source populations are not spatially interchangeable: their arrangement within the archipelago contains information beyond mainland isolation, source distance, source abundance, regional prevalence and generic island connectivity. At higher occupancy, the role of source information changes and may lose actual-topology specificity.

The conservation implication is deliberately framed as a testable hypothesis rather than an intervention claim. Connectivity assessment may need to be conditional on the occupancy state of the focal species; for ultrarare island species, preserving the spatial arrangement of remaining occupied populations may matter in ways that cannot be reduced to the number of populations alone. Our occurrence analysis does not demonstrate realized movement, recolonization or demographic rescue.

The study also reports the negative and non-estimable evidence transparently: the 5–12-presence layer did not replicate the overall effect, the broad near-ubiquitous layer was not estimable under its frozen pilot rule, a fresh boreal beetle test was non-supportive, and the plant fresh lane terminated without an ecological primary.

We believe this combination of global scale, sequential response sealing, matched topology nulls and explicit boundary results makes the manuscript well suited to GEB's emphasis on general biogeographic processes and macroecological inference.
